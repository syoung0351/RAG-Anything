# BATNA and Negotiation Frameworks

## BATNA — Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement

Developed by Roger Fisher and William Ury in “Getting to Yes” (1981). Your BATNA is what you will do if the negotiation fails to produce an agreement. It is not a threat — it is your floor.

**Before any negotiation, define:**
1. Your BATNA: the concrete alternative you will pursue if talks fail
2. Their likely BATNA: what they walk away to if this deal doesn’t close
3. Your reservation price: the worst deal you would accept before your BATNA looks better

Whoever has the stronger BATNA has more leverage. Improving your BATNA before negotiating is the most reliable way to increase your power.

**BATNA improvement tactics:**
- Develop competing offers before entering negotiation
- Make your alternatives more concrete and credible (signed LOIs, vendor quotes)
- Visibly demonstrate that you have options (without bluffing)

## ZOPA — Zone of Possible Agreement

The ZOPA is the range between your reservation price and theirs. If there is no overlap, no deal is possible.

```
Your floor ←—[  ZOPA  ]—→ Their ceiling
```

If you believe the ZOPA is narrow or non-existent:
- Explore whether the structure of the deal can be changed (payment timing, scope, risk allocation)
- Look for value trades: what is cheap for you to give but valuable to them? And vice versa?
- Consider whether you are negotiating with the right decision-maker

## Principled Negotiation (Interest-Based)

Fisher and Ury’s four principles:

1. **Separate the people from the problem.** Attack the issue, not the person.

2. **Focus on interests, not positions.** A position is what someone says they want. An interest is why they want it.
   - Example: “We need 120-day payment terms” (position). Why? Cash flow during implementation (interest). Can you solve the cash flow problem another way?

3. **Invent options for mutual gain.** Brainstorm before evaluating. Create a bigger pie before dividing it.

4. **Insist on objective criteria.** Use market rates, precedent, independent standards.

## Anchoring

The first number in a negotiation has outsized influence on the final outcome. Whoever anchors first typically wins.

**When to anchor:** When you have good information about the range. Always anchor first in salary and budget negotiations.

**Counter-anchoring:** Don’t counter immediately — pause, express surprise, then re-anchor with your own number.

## Common Negotiation Tactics and Counter-Tactics

| Tactic | How to counter |
|---|---|
| Flinch (visible surprise at your number) | Acknowledge but don’t move immediately. Explain the basis. |
| Good cop / bad cop | Name it. Ask to speak directly to whoever can decide. |
| False deadline | Test it. Ask what happens if you miss it. |
| Nibbling (asking for extras after deal is agreed) | Reopen the full negotiation. |
| Silence after your offer | Don’t fill the silence. First to speak loses. |
| Authority escalation | Pre-qualify authority before negotiating. |
